## Why Another Graphing Tool?

I love graphs. I find them fascinating, especially when they're designed in a minimalist style. A few lines, some carefully chosen colours and clear labels can make a set of numbers much more interesting to look at.

One of my inspirations was the graphs optimum tech uses in his videos on YouTube. I liked the way they looked, and it made me want to build my own graphing software.

There was also a more practical reason. Whenever I needed a graph for this blog, I was using Matplotlib. It worked, but I found the process cumbersome, particularly when I wanted to adjust how a graph looked. I wanted to be able to put in my data, change the styling while looking at the result, and export an image for a post.

So I made **[Grapho](https://grapho.akif.kr)**.

## From Data to a Graph

Grapho runs in the browser. You can import a CSV, TSV or JSON file, or paste your data straight into the editor. From there, you choose a graph type and the columns you want to show.

It supports line, area, scatter, vertical and horizontal bar charts, and pie charts. You can give each series its own colour, put it on the left or right Y axis, and adjust things such as line shape and smoothing.

The editor is split into six tabs: Setup, Series, Axes, Labels, Style and Animate. Most parts of the graph can also be edited by clicking them directly in the preview, so you can click an axis or a series to get to its settings.

## Getting the Look Right

The styling is a big part of why I wanted to make this in the first place.

I wanted control over the details that make a graph feel at home in a blog post: the background, colours, typeface, grid lines, labels and spacing. Grapho lets you adjust those alongside the graph's size, axis ranges and number formatting.

You can start with a Mosaic colour way or save your own. Mosaic is the design system I use for this website, and Grapho uses it too. The app's theme and the graph's colours are separate, so changing the editor's appearance doesn't change the image you're working on.

For my blog, the useful part is being able to make these adjustments while seeing the graph update. If a label needs more space or a grid line feels too prominent, I can change it there and then.

## Still Images and Animations

For a blog post, you can export a graph as SVG or PNG. SVG is useful when you want it to stay sharp at different sizes, while PNG gives you a straightforward image to drop into a page.

Grapho also supports animations. You build a sequence of steps that draw, fade or grow parts of the graph, or bring bars in one at a time. Each step has its own duration and easing, and can run after or alongside the previous step.

You can preview the animation, scrub through the timeline, and export it as MP4 or GIF.

## How It Works

Grapho is built with Svelte and Astro, with D3 utilities handling things such as scales and shapes.

At its centre is a renderer that takes the data, the graph's settings and a point in time, then returns an SVG. The preview, animation timeline and exports all use that same renderer. A still image is one rendered frame; an animation is a sequence of them.

MP4 export uses WebCodecs through Mediabunny, while GIF export uses gifenc. The graph rendering and exports happen in your browser, without uploading your data to a server.

Charts are saved in the browser's local storage and can be reopened from the Charts menu. That also means they're tied to that browser: they don't sync between devices, and clearing the site's storage removes them.
