## Introduction

I've been using a K+DCAN cable for diagnostics and coding on my BMW, and decided to give it a detachable USB-C connection.

The idea is pretty simple: remove the fixed USB-A lead and put a USB-C socket in its place. Then I can use a separate USB cable rather than having one permanently attached to the adapter.

Of course, that also meant finding a way to hold the new socket in the old case. A good excuse to design another little 3D printed part.

## Opening It Up

The case is held together by four screws, two on either side. Removing those lets the case open up to reveal the PCB.

<PostImage src="/static/images/kdcan-usbc-conversion/02-open-case.jpg" alt="K+DCAN adapter opened to reveal the PCB, with the four case screws removed" subtitle="Four screws removed, two on either side, to open the case" />

Inside, the original cable had four wires going to a four-pin JST-XH-style plug, fitted onto bare header pins on the PCB.

That made the wiring side fairly convenient. I could remove the old plug and cable, then make up a replacement lead between the existing header and the USB-C board.

## Choosing the USB-C Socket

I went for a compact USB-C female USB 2.0 socket board with integrated CC pull-down resistors. It has the four connections I needed for this conversion: `VBUS`, `D-`, `D+` and `GND`.

The integrated resistors were one of the reasons I chose this board, rather than adding those separately in the already limited space inside the case.

The idea is to keep the adapter's existing electronics and just replace the fixed lead with a socket.

## Making the New Lead

I bought a prewired four-pin JST-XH connector rather than making up the whole thing from individual terminals.

<PostImage src="/static/images/kdcan-usbc-conversion/04-connector-comparison.jpg" alt="Original and replacement four-pin connectors side by side, with different wire colours and layouts" subtitle="Original lead on the left, replacement on the right" />

<PostImage src="/static/images/kdcan-usbc-conversion/05-new-connector.jpg" alt="Complete new prewired four-pin JST-XH connector before modification" subtitle="The replacement connector before depinning" />

The wires weren't in the order I needed, so I depinned the connector and moved them around to match the labelled pinout on the K+DCAN PCB. I went by the labels on the board, rather than assuming the wire colours or the supplied order would match.

<PostImage src="/static/images/kdcan-usbc-conversion/06-depinned-connector.jpg" alt="Four wires removed from the new JST-XH housing with their crimp terminals attached" subtitle="The replacement connector depinned, ready to reorder the wires" />

On mine, the PCB is labelled `5V`, `D+`, `D-`, `GND`. After reordering, those correspond to red, yellow, white and black on the new lead.

<PostImage src="/static/images/kdcan-usbc-conversion/07-reordered-connector.jpg" alt="Reordered four-pin connector plugged into PCB pins labelled 5V, D+, D- and GND" subtitle="Reordered to match the PCB: 5V, D+, D-, GND" />

I then shortened the wires to suit the space inside the case.

<div class="[&_img]:aspect-[3/1] [&_img]:object-cover [&_img]:object-[50%_68%]">
  <PostImage src="/static/images/kdcan-usbc-conversion/08-shortened-wires.jpg" alt="Shortened four-wire JST lead next to the USB-C socket board" subtitle="The shortened lead next to the USB-C board" />
</div>

With the wires cut to length, I soldered them onto the USB-C board using the following connections:

| K+DCAN PCB connection | USB-C board pad |
| --- | --- |
| `5V` (red) | `VBUS` |
| `D+` (yellow) | `D+` |
| `D-` (white) | `D-` |
| `GND` (black) | `GND` |

That's the signal mapping I used, rather than a numbered pin order to copy. The order needs to match the labels on the actual adapter PCB.

## Insulation & Continuity Checks

With the soldering done, I applied liquid electrical tape to insulate the connections.

I also checked the wiring for continuity and checked between the individual pins for shorts. I didn't find any unintended continuity between the separate connections.

Ground also had continuity to the metal shell of this USB-C socket. That's an observation from this particular board, rather than something I'd assume about every USB-C socket.

## Repairing the K-Line Switch Wiring

While working on the adapter, the original wires for the K-Line switch snapped off. The wires fitted by the manufacturer were very flimsy, so I replaced them using the leftover lengths from the new JST lead.

I resoldered the switch wiring and used liquid electrical tape to secure it again.

To check it, I used my multimeter between pins 7 and 8 of the OBD connector with the switch engaged and disengaged. I only had continuity between those pins in K-Line mode, confirming that the switch bridged pins 7 and 8 only in that position.

## The 3D Printed Jacket

The original cable had a strain relief where it left the case. Removing the cable left that space available for the new socket, but I still needed something to retain it.

My solution is a little 3D printed "jacket" to hold the USB-C board in place and replace the original strain relief.

The aim is for the printed part to take the force of plugging and unplugging a cable, rather than leaving that to the wires and solder joints.

<PostImage src="/static/images/kdcan-usbc-conversion/jacket-cad.png" alt="CAD render of the retaining jacket with a raised circular face and a rounded USB-C opening" subtitle="The jacket I designed to replace the original cable strain relief" />

Here's the sketch I drew up with all the dimensions. The outer shell, minus the USB-C opening, was extruded 4.8mm. The circular bump was then extruded a further 1.6mm, with a 4.8mm starting offset so it sits on top of the shell.

<PostImage src="/static/images/kdcan-usbc-conversion/jacket-sketch.png" alt="Dimensioned CAD sketch of the jacket: a 14.5mm square with R1 corners, an 11.2mm diameter circle, and an 8.75mm by 3.05mm USB-C opening with R1.2 corners" subtitle="The sketch for the jacket, with all the dimensions" />

I also used liquid electrical tape to glue the wires to the jacket and keep the lead in place.

## Assembly & Testing

With the jacket pushed into the original cable opening, the USB-C socket and replacement lead sit inside the case.

### One Final Wiring Check

Before plugging it into a computer, I sacrificed a USB-A-to-C cable that I knew only had four wires. I cut off the Type-A end, stripped back the outer insulation and exposed the ends of all four individual wires.

With the USB-C end plugged into the adapter, I checked continuity from each exposed cable wire to the corresponding wire on the JST lead:

| Cable wire | JST wire | Continuity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Red | Red (`5V`) | Yes |
| Green | Yellow (`D+`) | Yes |
| White | White (`D-`) | Yes |
| Black | Black (`GND`) | Yes |

All four matched. This checked the wiring through the cable and USB-C connection to the JST lead, rather than just checking the solder joints individually.

### Testing on the Laptop

With the wiring checks done, I plugged the modified adapter into my laptop. It was recognised, and I could see it in INPA with the two dots flashing.

<PostImage src="/static/images/kdcan-usbc-conversion/14-final-build.jpg" alt="USB-C conversion fitted inside the K+DCAN adapter, with the case lid and screws beside it" subtitle="Everything fitted together inside the case" />

### Testing in the Car

Finally, I tested it in the car. With a long USB cable, the adapter didn't want to show up on my laptop. I'm not sure whether that was down to the cable or something else.

A shorter coiled cable I already had in the car worked, and I was able to communicate with the car using INPA without any issues.
